Why it mattersThe estate's solicitor is the person who instructs the sale. You're reading their name and number months before the family talks to an agent.

"But it's a deceased estate — won't the family just keep it?"

Fair question. Three things you should know before you decide this isn't for you.

1. Probate is the starting gun, not the finish line.

When someone dies owning property, the executor can't sell or transfer the house until the Supreme Court of NSW grants probate. Before applying, their solicitor must publish a public notice. That notice is the earliest public moment an estate starts moving — and it's what this report captures, the day it appears.

2. "Goes to next of kin" means the value — not the house.

Beneficiaries inherit the estate's value. Three siblings can't live in one house, so a $7m Dover Heights property almost always has to become cash to be split. Most beneficiaries are in their 50s and 60s with homes and mortgages of their own. An inherited clifftop house is an illiquid windfall, not a home.

3. There's a tax clock running.

Sell an inherited main residence within two years of the date of death and the sale is generally CGT-exempt. Hold it longer and that exemption can be lost. Add insurance, rates, land-tax exposure and an empty house bleeding money, and the keep-it option gets expensive fast. Some families do keep the property — which is why this is sold as six signals, not six listings. The maths above already prices that in.
